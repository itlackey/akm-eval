# The Maillard Reaction

The Maillard reaction is a chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing
sugars that occurs when food is heated, producing hundreds of new flavor and
aroma compounds along with the characteristic brown crust on seared meat, baked
bread, and roasted coffee.

Conditions that favor it:

- **Dry surface** — moisture has to evaporate before the surface temperature can
  climb past 100°C (212°F); water on the surface caps the temperature and delays
  browning. Pat proteins dry before searing.
- **High heat** — the reaction accelerates sharply above roughly 140–165°C
  (285–330°F), which is well above a gentle simmer.
- **Enough surface contact and time** — a crowded pan drops the local
  temperature and traps steam, which is the single most common reason a home
  sear turns gray instead of brown (see the "don't crowd the pan" lesson).
- **A pinch of alkalinity** helps in some contexts (baking, or a pinch of baking
  soda on stir-fry meat) because Maillard chemistry runs faster at higher pH.

Maillard browning is distinct from caramelization, which is the browning of
sugars alone without amino acids and requires even higher temperatures.
