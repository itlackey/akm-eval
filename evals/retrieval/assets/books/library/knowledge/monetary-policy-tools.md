# Monetary Policy Tools
Central banks manage the money supply and short-term interest rates using
three principal tools. Open market operations — buying or selling government
securities — are the primary day-to-day lever: purchases inject reserves
into the banking system and push short-term rates down, while sales drain
reserves and push rates up. The policy interest rate (for example the
federal funds rate target in the United States) signals the central bank's
stance and influences borrowing costs throughout the economy, from mortgages
to corporate bonds. Reserve requirements, which mandate the minimum reserves
banks must hold against deposits, are a blunter and less frequently used
tool in modern practice. In expansionary policy, central banks lower rates
and buy securities to encourage borrowing and spending during a downturn; in
contractionary policy, they raise rates and sell securities to cool an
overheating, inflationary economy. Since the 2008 financial crisis, central
banks have also used large-scale asset purchases (quantitative easing) to
ease conditions once short-term rates approach zero.
