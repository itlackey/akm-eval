# Contract Formation Elements
Under common law, an enforceable contract generally requires four elements:
offer, acceptance, consideration, and mutual intent to be bound. An offer is
a manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain on specific terms,
communicated to an offeree in a way that invites acceptance. Acceptance is an
unequivocal assent to those exact terms; a reply that changes any term is
usually a counteroffer, not an acceptance, under the mirror-image rule.
Consideration is the bargained-for exchange of value — each side must give
up something of legal value, whether money, a promise, or forbearance from
an act one is otherwise entitled to do. Courts also look for the parties'
objective intent to be legally bound, judged by what a reasonable person
would understand from the words and conduct, not by an undisclosed
subjective intent. Contracts lacking any one of these elements are typically
void or voidable, though doctrines like promissory estoppel can sometimes
substitute for missing consideration when a party reasonably relied on a
promise to their detriment.
