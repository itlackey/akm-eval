# Innate vs. Adaptive Immunity

The immune system has two cooperating arms that differ in speed,
specificity, and memory.

## Innate immunity

Present from birth, responds within minutes to hours, and does not improve
with repeated exposure.

- **Physical/chemical barriers** — skin, mucus, stomach acid, lysozyme in
  tears and saliva.
- **Cellular defenders** — neutrophils and macrophages phagocytose pathogens;
  natural killer (NK) cells destroy virus-infected and tumor cells; mast
  cells release histamine to drive inflammation.
- **Soluble defenses** — complement proteins opsonize pathogens and can lyse
  them directly; interferons signal neighboring cells to raise antiviral
  defenses.
- **Inflammation** — the coordinated vascular and cellular response (redness,
  heat, swelling, pain) that recruits immune cells to a site of injury or
  infection.

## Adaptive immunity

Develops over days, is highly specific to a given antigen, and produces
immunological memory so re-exposure triggers a faster, stronger response.

- **Humoral immunity (B cells)** — B cells differentiate into plasma cells
  that secrete antibodies specific to an antigen; some become long-lived
  memory B cells.
- **Cell-mediated immunity (T cells)** — helper T cells (CD4+) coordinate the
  response by activating B cells and macrophages; cytotoxic T cells (CD8+)
  directly kill infected cells; memory T cells persist for rapid recall.

## Why vaccination works

Vaccines expose the adaptive immune system to a harmless form or fragment of
a pathogen, generating memory B and T cells without causing disease, so a
real future exposure is met with a faster, larger response.
